Peridot: August's Birthstone — The Gem Born in Fire

Aug 16, 2026 7 min read

While most gemstones are formed within the Earth's crust, peridot boasts an origin story far more extreme. The vibrant, golden-green birthstone for August is a true extreme gem, born deep within the Earth's fiery mantle and delivered to the surface by violent volcanic eruptions. If that isn't otherworldly enough, peridot is also one of the rare gemstones found in meteorites that have traveled through the freezing vacuum of space. Let's delve into the fascinating world of this extraordinary green jewel.

What is Peridot?

Peridot (pronounced pear-ih-doh or pear-ih-dot) is the gem-quality variety of the mineral olivine. Unlike many gemstones that occur in a rainbow of colors, peridot is idiochromatic, meaning its color is an inherent part of its chemical composition rather than the result of trace impurities. Specifically, it is the iron in peridot's crystal structure that gives it its signature yellowish-green color.

"Peridot is one of the few gemstones that occurs in only one color: an olive-green. The intensity and tint of the green, however, depend on the percentage of iron in the crystal structure."

The color can range from a yellow-green to a brownish-green, but the most sought-after peridots are a bright, lime green with no brown hints. These top-color stones generally possess an iron content of less than 15% and often contain traces of chromium and nickel.

Born in the Mantle and Outer Space

Geologically speaking, peridot is remarkable. It forms deep in the Earth's mantle (roughly 20 to 55 miles below the surface) under extreme heat and pressure. The peridot we mine today was brought to the surface millions of years ago by deep-source volcanic eruptions. The crystals are often found embedded in basalt rock, the cooled remnants of volcanic magma.

But Earth is not the only source of peridot. Pallasite meteorites, which are thought to be the remnants of the core-mantle boundary of an ancient asteroid, contain beautiful, glowing green peridot crystals embedded in a nickel-iron matrix. Extraterrestrial peridot is exceptionally rare and highly prized by collectors.

Additionally, volcanic action on Earth has created rare phenomena, such as Papakolea Beach in Hawaii, famously known as the "Green Sand Beach." The sand here gets its distinct color from countless tiny grains of olivine (peridot) that were washed out of a nearby cinder cone.

Historical Significance: Cleopatra's "Emeralds"

Peridot has been revered for thousands of years. The ancient Egyptians called it the "gem of the sun" and mined it on a desolate, snake-infested island in the Red Sea called Topazios (now known as Zabargad Island). This island remained the primary source of fine peridot for over 3,500 years.

Historians now believe that many of the famous "emeralds" worn by Cleopatra were actually fine peridots from Zabargad. In the ancient world, many green stones were casually grouped together, and the rich, glowing green of top-quality peridot could easily have rivaled the emeralds of the time.

Modern Sources of Peridot

While Zabargad is historically significant, modern peridot comes from several diverse locations across the globe:

  • San Carlos Apache Indian Reservation, Arizona: One of the world's major suppliers of peridot. The stones here are typically smaller but have excellent color.
  • Pakistan (Suppat Region): Discovered in the 1990s high in the Himalayan Mountains. Pakistani peridot is renowned for producing large, magnificent crystals with a rich, vibrant green hue. It represents some of the finest peridot on the market today.
  • Myanmar (Burma): Known for producing large, beautifully colored peridots, often found as loose crystals in the soil.
  • China: A significant contemporary producer of commercial-grade peridot.

Optical Phenomenon: Double Refraction

One of the most identifiable characteristics of peridot is its strong double refraction. When you look closely into a large peridot, especially through the table (the flat top facet), you can often see the facets on the pavilion (the bottom of the stone) appearing doubled. This optical trait gives peridot a deep, slightly sleepy, and glowing appearance that sets it apart from other green stones like emerald or tourmaline.

Care and Maintenance

With a Mohs hardness of 6.5 to 7, peridot is slightly softer than gems like sapphire or diamond. It is suitable for everyday wear in earrings and pendants, but peridot rings require a bit more care to prevent scratching or chipping. It is also susceptible to sudden temperature changes and should never be cleaned in an ultrasonic or steam cleaner. Warm, soapy water and a soft brush are the safest cleaning methods.

Whether it fell from the sky in a meteorite or exploded from the earth in a volcano, peridot is a gemstone of phenomenal origins. Its bright, joyful color makes it an enduring favorite, and a perfect birthstone to celebrate the height of summer in August.
