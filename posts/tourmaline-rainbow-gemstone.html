Tourmaline: The Rainbow Gemstone — Every Color Imaginable

Aug 17, 2026 8 min read

If you were to gather one of every gemstone in the world, the tourmaline family alone could recreate the entire spectrum of the rainbow. There is an old Egyptian legend that explains this phenomenon: on its long journey up from the center of the Earth, tourmaline passed over a rainbow, assuming all of its colors. Today, gemologists know the true science behind its hues, but the sheer variety of tourmaline remains nothing short of magical.

What is Tourmaline?

Tourmaline is not a single mineral, but a complex group of closely related boron silicate minerals. Because its chemical composition can accommodate a vast array of trace elements—including iron, magnesium, copper, manganese, and lithium—tourmaline crystallizes in a wider range of colors than any other gem species.

"Tourmaline's name is derived from the Sinhalese word 'toramalli,' which means 'stone with mixed colors.' It's a fitting title for a gem that can mimic almost any other stone."

The main species of tourmaline used in jewelry include Elbaite (which provides most of the gem-quality colorful stones), Schorl (opaque black), Dravite (brown), and Liddicoatite (famous for intricate color zoning and slicing).

The Star of the Show: Paraíba Tourmaline

In the late 1980s, the gemstone world was rocked by the discovery of a new type of tourmaline in the Brazilian state of Paraíba. These stones exhibited intense, glowing "neon" blue and green colors never before seen in any gemstone. The secret to their incredible hue? Copper.

Paraíba tourmaline is so intensely colored that it appears to glow from within, even in low light. Because the original Brazilian mines are virtually depleted, genuine Paraíba tourmalines are astronomically expensive, often fetching tens of thousands of dollars per carat. Similar copper-bearing tourmalines have since been found in Mozambique and Nigeria, ensuring the continued, albeit scarce, supply of this mesmerizing gem.

A Spectrum of Varieties

Because it comes in so many colors, the gem trade has created specific names for the different varieties of tourmaline:

  • Rubellite: A vivid, highly saturated pink, red, or purplish-red tourmaline. Fine rubellite can rival the color of fine ruby.
  • Indicolite: A deep, rich blue or greenish-blue tourmaline. Pure blue indicolite is quite rare and valuable.
  • Chrome Tourmaline: A vibrant, intense green tourmaline colored by chromium and vanadium (the same elements that color emerald). It is usually found in East Africa.
  • Watermelon Tourmaline: A spectacular bi-color or tri-color stone that features a pink center surrounded by an outer layer of green, perfectly mimicking a slice of watermelon.
  • Schorl: Black tourmaline. While not often faceted for fine jewelry, it is incredibly popular in crystal healing and alternative therapies for its grounding properties.

Bi-Color and Multi-Color Crystals

One of the most fascinating aspects of tourmaline is its ability to display multiple colors in a single crystal. This happens when the trace elements in the fluid surrounding the growing crystal change over time. Crystals can be bi-color, tri-color, or even have complex geometric color zoning (especially in the Liddicoatite variety) that is revealed when the crystal is sliced in cross-sections.

Tourmaline Properties

Property Details
Mineral Group Complex Boron Silicates (Elbaite, Schorl, etc.)
Mohs Hardness 7 to 7.5 (Good for everyday jewelry)
Color Range Every color; bi-color and multi-color common
Unique Traits Piezoelectric and Pyroelectric
Major Sources Brazil, Afghanistan, Africa, USA (Maine/California)

Electric Properties: Piezoelectricity and Pyroelectricity

Beyond its beauty, tourmaline has unique physical properties. It is both pyroelectric and piezoelectric. This means that when a tourmaline crystal is heated (pyro) or placed under physical pressure (piezo), it generates a weak electrical charge, becoming polarized with a positive charge at one end and a negative charge at the other.

Historically, the Dutch utilized this property. They called the stone "Aschentrekker" (ash puller) and used heated tourmaline crystals to pull the ash out of their meerschaum pipes.

Historic American Deposits

While Brazil and Africa are the major contemporary producers, the United States holds a proud place in tourmaline history. In the late 19th and early 20th centuries, significant deposits of spectacular pink and green tourmaline were discovered in Maine and Southern California. In fact, the Empress Dowager Cixi of China was so enamored with California pink tourmaline that she bought massive quantities of it, sustaining the San Diego County mining industry for years.
