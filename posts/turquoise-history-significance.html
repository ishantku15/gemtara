Turquoise: 8,000 Years of History, Power & Significance

Aug 2, 2026 8 min read

Turquoise is a stone of the ancients, carrying over 8,000 years of history, power, and significance. Its unmistakable robin's egg blue color has captivated pharaohs, kings, and Native American shamans alike.

As a copper aluminum phosphate mineral, turquoise forms in arid regions where water percolates through host rocks, interacting with copper and phosphorus. The study of this fascinating gemstone reveals deep complexities that have intrigued humanity for millennia. When examining the physical and metaphysical properties, we uncover a rich tapestry of history and gemological science. The intricate crystalline structures and trace elements interact with light in ways that defy simple explanation, creating the visual marvels we appreciate today.

8,000 Years of History

The history of turquoise is as rich as its color. Persian turquoise, known for its pristine blue without matrix, set the standard for quality for millennia. In ancient Egypt, turquoise was carved into scarabs and adorned the iconic burial mask of King Tutankhamun.

Throughout various civilizations, from ancient empires to modern bespoke jewelry designers, the cultural significance remains steadfast. Furthermore, ongoing geological discoveries continue to challenge our understanding of its formation processes deep within the Earth's crust, proving that nature is indeed the most remarkable artist.

Native American Traditions

In the American Southwest, turquoise is deeply woven into the spiritual and artistic fabric of Native American tribes, including the Navajo, Zuni, and Hopi. It represents water, the sky, and bountiful harvests. The intricate silverwork combined with turquoise stones is highly sought after by collectors worldwide.

Famous Mines: The Sleeping Beauty

One of the most legendary sources of turquoise was the Sleeping Beauty mine in Arizona. Known for its solid, light blue color with little to no matrix, Sleeping Beauty turquoise is considered the benchmark for American turquoise. Although the mine is now closed, its stones remain highly prized.

Treatments and Stabilization

Natural turquoise is often porous and can absorb oils or change color over time. To make the stone suitable for everyday jewelry, the vast majority of turquoise on the market undergoes stabilization—a process where the stone is infused with clear epoxy resin under pressure.

Mohave Turquoise

Mohave turquoise is a stabilized and color-enhanced product, often dyed vibrant purple, green, or bright blue, and sometimes infused with bronze or copper matrix. While striking, it is important to know that it is heavily treated.

Spotting Fakes and Grading

Due to its popularity, fake turquoise is rampant. Materials like dyed howlite, magnesite, or even plastic are frequently sold as the real deal. True turquoise feels cool to the touch and has a specific density. Grading turquoise involves assessing its color, the presence and attractiveness of the matrix (the host rock webbing), and its hardness.
