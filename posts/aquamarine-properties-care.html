Aquamarine: Properties, Meaning & Care Guide

Aug 10, 2026 8 min read

Gazing into an aquamarine is like looking into the crystal-clear waters of a pristine tropical ocean. This mesmerizing gemstone, deeply intertwined with the lore of the sea, has captivated humanity for centuries with its tranquil blue to greenish-blue hues. As the traditional birthstone for March, aquamarine is celebrated not just for its stunning appearance but also for its rich history, purported mystical properties, and robust physical characteristics. In this comprehensive guide, we will dive deep into everything you need to know about aquamarine—from its mineral composition and historical significance to expert advice on how to care for your precious stones.

The Geological and Chemical Roots of Aquamarine

Aquamarine belongs to the prestigious beryl family of minerals, making it a close cousin to the lush green emerald and the delicate pink morganite. The fundamental chemical composition of beryl is beryllium aluminum cyclosilicate. While pure beryl is colorless, the presence of trace elements during the crystal's formation gives rise to its colorful varieties.

The enchanting blue color of aquamarine is primarily caused by trace amounts of iron (specifically ferrous and ferric iron) within the beryl crystal lattice. The specific valency and concentration of these iron ions dictate the exact shade, which can range from a pale, barely-there blue to a deeply saturated oceanic teal.

The Coveted Santa Maria Aquamarine

Among the various shades available, the Santa Maria aquamarine stands as the pinnacle of color saturation. Named after the Santa Maria de Itabira mine in Minas Gerais, Brazil, where these stones were originally discovered, this variety exhibits an intensely saturated blue that is highly sought after by collectors and connoisseurs. Today, similarly colored stones found in other locations, such as Mozambique and Madagascar, are often referred to as "Santa Maria Africana" to denote their exceptional quality.

Heat Treatment: Enhancing the Ocean's Hue

It is a well-established practice within the gemstone industry to subject aquamarine to controlled heat treatment. Natural aquamarine often features a noticeable greenish tint. While some purists prefer the unadulterated "sea-green" appearance, the modern market generally favors a pure, crystalline blue.

By heating the stones to temperatures between 400°C and 450°C, the ferric iron, which is responsible for the yellow/green hues, is reduced to ferrous iron. This process permanently removes the green tint, leaving behind a pure, stable blue color. This treatment is standard, widely accepted, and does not compromise the structural integrity of the stone.

Myths, Legends, and the March Birthstone

The name "aquamarine" is derived from the Latin words aqua (water) and marina (of the sea). Unsurprisingly, its lore is deeply connected to maritime traditions.

"Ancient sailors believed that aquamarine was the treasure of mermaids, carrying it as a talisman to calm the waves and guarantee safe passage across treacherous oceans."

Beyond its maritime associations, aquamarine has long been considered a symbol of youth, hope, health, and fidelity. As the modern birthstone for March, it is often given as a gift to celebrate spring birthdays, as well as the 19th wedding anniversary. Its calming color is thought to soothe the temper, facilitate clear communication, and bring harmony to relationships.

Properties of Aquamarine

Aquamarine is an excellent gemstone for everyday wear, thanks to its physical durability and lack of cleavage (a gemological term referring to the tendency of a crystal to break along specific structural planes).

Aquamarine Value Factors

Like all colored gemstones, the value of an aquamarine is determined by a combination of the Four Cs: Color, Clarity, Cut, and Carat weight.

  • Color: The most crucial factor. The highest values are assigned to stones with a deep, intensely saturated blue with little to no green modifier. Lighter shades are more common and therefore more affordable.
  • Clarity: Unlike its cousin emerald, which is almost always heavily included, aquamarine is typically "eye-clean." This means that inclusions should not be visible to the naked eye. Stones with visible flaws are significantly devalued.
  • Cut: Aquamarine is often cut into brilliant or step cuts (like the emerald cut) to maximize its natural luster and showcase its clarity. A well-proportioned cut is essential for bringing out the stone's brilliance.
  • Carat: Aquamarine crystals can grow to enormous sizes in nature. While larger stones are more valuable overall, the price per carat does not increase exponentially with size as it does with diamonds or rubies, unless the color is exceptionally saturated.

Comprehensive Care Guide

With a hardness of 7.5 to 8, aquamarine is durable enough for daily wear in rings, bracelets, and necklaces. However, it still requires proper care to maintain its pristine appearance over the years.

Cleaning Routine

The safest and most effective way to clean aquamarine jewelry is with warm, soapy water and a soft-bristled brush (like a baby toothbrush). Gently scrub behind the stone where dirt and oils tend to accumulate, which can dull the gem's brilliance. Ultrasonic and steam cleaners are generally safe for aquamarine, provided the stone does not have liquid inclusions or fractures, which could cause it to crack under extreme pressure or temperature changes.

Storage and Wear

Store aquamarine jewelry separately from harder stones, such as diamonds or sapphires, which can scratch it. Keep it in a soft pouch or a fabric-lined jewelry box. Avoid exposing your aquamarine to harsh chemicals, including hydrofluoric acid, household cleaners, and even prolonged exposure to intense heat, which could potentially cause thermal shock.

Aquamarine remains a timeless favorite among gem enthusiasts and jewelry lovers alike. Its serene color, excellent durability, and fascinating history make it a gemstone worthy of admiration and a prominent place in any collection.
