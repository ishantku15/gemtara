Alexandrite: The Chameleon Gem — Emerald by Day, Ruby by Night

Aug 17, 2026 9 min read

In the vast realm of gemstones, few hold the mystique and sheer jaw-dropping magic of alexandrite. Often described by gemologists as "emerald by day, ruby by night," this extraordinary stone changes color based on the ambient lighting. It is one of the rarest, most highly prized, and consequently, most expensive gemstones in the world, often surpassing diamonds in per-carat value. Let's delve into the science, history, and allure of the chameleon gem.

The Discovery in the Russian Urals

The story of alexandrite is deeply intertwined with Russian imperial history. The gemstone was first discovered in the 1830s in the emerald mines near the Tokovaya River in the Ural Mountains of Russia. According to popular legend, Finnish mineralogist Nils Gustaf Nordenskiöld was examining what he thought was an unusual emerald. Under the sunlight, it was a brilliant green, but when he examined it later that evening by the light of a candle, the stone had turned a striking purplish-red.

"Because its red and green colors matched the military colors of Imperial Russia, the gem was named 'Alexandrite' in honor of the future Tsar Alexander II, who came of age on the very day the gemstone was reportedly found."

The original Ural Mountain deposits produced stones with a dramatic, highly saturated color change. However, these historic Russian mines were largely depleted by the end of the 19th century. Today, antique Russian alexandrite jewelry is exceedingly rare and commands astronomical prices at auction.

How the Color Change Works

Alexandrite is a rare variety of the mineral chrysoberyl. What sets it apart from ordinary chrysoberyl is the presence of the trace element chromium—the exact same element responsible for the green of emeralds and the red of rubies. But how can one element cause a stone to be both green and red?

The secret lies in alexandrite's unique crystal structure and its absorption spectrum. The stone strongly absorbs light in the yellow region of the visible light spectrum while transmitting large amounts of green and red light. Human vision is highly sensitive to green light, so in daylight or fluorescent light (which is rich in green and blue wavelengths), the stone appears green to bluish-green. However, incandescent light (like candlelight or old-fashioned lightbulbs) is rich in red wavelengths and poor in blue/green. Under this light, the stone transmits the red wavelengths to the eye, appearing red to purplish-red.

Global Sources: Brazil, Sri Lanka, and Beyond

For decades after the Russian mines ran dry, the gem world thought alexandrite was all but lost. Then, in 1987, a major new deposit was discovered in Hematita, Minas Gerais, Brazil. Brazilian alexandrite is celebrated for displaying a color change that rivals the legendary Russian stones, shifting from a crisp bluish-green to a rich raspberry red.

Sri Lanka (Ceylon) is another significant producer. Sri Lankan alexandrites tend to be larger but often have less saturated colors, shifting from a yellowish or brownish-green to a brownish-red. More recently, deposits in Tanzania, Madagascar, and Zimbabwe have added to the global supply, though fine quality remains exceptionally scarce.

The Rarity and Value Factor

Natural alexandrite is incredibly rare. The geological conditions required for its formation—beryllium and chromium occurring together with a lack of silica—are highly unusual. Because of this, large stones are practically non-existent. Most faceted alexandrites weigh less than one carat. A natural, high-quality alexandrite over one carat can easily fetch tens of thousands of dollars per carat, far outpricing equivalent diamonds or sapphires.

Value is determined primarily by the strength and quality of the color change. A stone that exhibits a 100% color shift from pure green to pure red is the holy grail. Clarity is also important, but gemologists will tolerate more inclusions in alexandrite than in other gems, provided the color change is spectacular.

Cat's Eye Alexandrite

As if color-change wasn't phenomenal enough, alexandrite can occasionally exhibit a second optical phenomenon: chatoyancy, or the "cat's eye" effect. When the stone contains parallel needle-like inclusions and is cut as a cabochon (domed), light reflects off the inclusions to create a bright line down the center, resembling the slit eye of a cat. A cat's eye alexandrite that also changes color is an extreme rarity in the gemological world.

Lab-Created vs. Natural Alexandrite

Because natural alexandrite is so expensive and rare, the market is flooded with synthetics. Lab-created alexandrite is grown using various methods (flux-grown, Czochralski pull) and shares the exact chemical and optical properties of the natural stone. These are genuine chrysoberyls and exhibit a stunning color change.

However, beware of cheap imitations sold in souvenir shops or online. These are often color-change synthetic corundum (sapphire) or color-change glass. A trusted gemological laboratory report (like GIA or AGS) is absolutely essential when buying natural alexandrite.

Alexandrite represents the pinnacle of gemological rarity. It is a stone that seemingly defies the laws of nature, offering the wearer two completely different, exquisite jewels bound within a single, highly durable crystal.
